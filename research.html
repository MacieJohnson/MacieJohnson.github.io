Research Statement

“Mining of the periodic table and classic organic literature are two avenues that are likely to bear interesting fruit.”

-Carolyn Bertozzi

Bertozzi describes certain avenues of research as likely to “bear interesting fruit.” The relationship between light exposure and myopia is one such avenue. Given that a growing body of evidence exists yet results still differ by species and time of day, clear room remains for new discoveries. The prospect of those discoveries is therefore what first drew me to ophthalmology. Conversations with ophthalmologists have shown me that the eye is not merely an organ of vision but a window onto systemic health, in which the detection of anomalies can reveal serious underlying conditions, including cancer. As a result of this recognition, my interest has been directed toward the relationship between environmental factors and ocular development, with particular attention to myopia. Consequently, I hope to contribute to a laboratory investigating how the wavelength and timing of diurnal light exposure influence ocular growth. The public-health stakes of this line of inquiry are underscored by Holden et al. (2016), who projected that by 2050, 50% of the world’s population will have myopia and 10% will have high myopia, representing a “2-fold increase in myopia prevalence” and a “5-fold increase in high myopia prevalence” compared to 2000.

Myopia results from excessive axial elongation of the eye, and research in Investigative Ophthalmology & Visual Science indicates that the eye uses environmental photic cues to regulate that growth. Recent studies have shown that the wavelength and timing of light can modulate axial length in humans and animals, though results differ depending on the species and the time of day. I am interested in discovering what, if any, causal relationship exists between the wavelength and timing of diurnal light exposure and ocular growth in children. Further research in this field has the potential to yield preventative light-based strategies for myopia, an advancement compared to current optical corrections, which compensate for refractive error only after the onset of myopia.
